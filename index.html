01ECON 355  ·  Spring 2026  ·  Collaborative research project

Network Economics and Competitive Strategy in Global Airline Alliances

Network analysis  /  Pricing strategy  /  Market structure  /  Consumer welfare

The question

How do different airline-network structures shape competitive strategy, pricing, revenue optimization, and consumer welfare?

The approach

Compared the three major global airline alliances, Star Alliance, SkyTeam and Oneworld, using concepts from network economics, game theory, pricing theory, market segmentation and welfare economics. The analysis examined how multi-hub-and-spoke, hybrid, and selectively concentrated networks correspond with different business strategies and customer outcomes.

Star Alliance

Scale and global connectivity through a large multi-hub-and-spoke system.

SkyTeam

Connectivity balanced with flexibility through a more hybrid structure.

Oneworld

Concentration on high-value routes and premium travelers, prioritizing yield per passenger over maximum coverage.

Key finding

Network structure creates a fundamental economic tradeoff. Greater connectivity and coordination can improve efficiency, expand consumer access and increase network value. The same structures can also increase market power, support price discrimination and reduce competition on concentrated routes.

Josephine’s focus

  • Customer matching and segmentation
  • Origin-destination pricing and revenue optimization
  • Network utilization and economies of density
  • Consumer surplus and welfare effects
  • Market power and price discrimination
  • Comparative synthesis across the three alliance models

Economic concepts applied

  • Hub-and-spoke systems
  • Origin-destination pricing
  • Price discrimination
  • Economies of density
  • Market power
  • Consumer surplus
  • Welfare analysis

02ECON 358  ·  Spring 2026  ·  Collaborative research project

Economic Policy Analysis: Hawaiʻi’s Solid Waste System

Market failure  /  Externalities  /  Policy design  /  Feasibility analysis

The question

How can Hawaiʻi address the economic and environmental costs of solid-waste disposal within the constraints of an isolated island economy?

The approach

Analyzed Hawaiʻi’s solid-waste system as a market-failure problem shaped by negative externalities, geographic isolation, limited landfill capacity, high transportation costs, public-goods and free-rider problems, and limited competition. The project evaluated the state’s existing waste-management infrastructure and developed policy and investment alternatives using a circular-economy framework.

The linear chain leaves disposal costs outside the firm’s decision. A producer fee routes them back into it.

Proposed solutions

Extended Producer Responsibility for packaging. A producer-fee system designed to make packaging recyclability and waste generation part of firms’ economic incentives, while generating revenue for improved recycling infrastructure.

Investment in H-Cycle infrastructure. A longer-term infrastructure strategy intended to reduce reliance on landfills while creating economic value from waste streams.

Feasibility analysis

The project went beyond identifying an economically desirable solution and examined the constraints on actually implementing it:

  • Up-front infrastructure costs
  • Producer incentives
  • Collective versus individual producer-responsibility models
  • Political resistance to prior legislation
  • Existing state policy initiatives
  • Legal constraints including the Dormant Commerce Clause
  • Hawaiʻi’s geographic and infrastructure limitations
“An economically attractive environmental policy is not necessarily an implementable one.”

Key insight

Effective waste policy in Hawaiʻi must address externalities while also accounting for producer incentives, infrastructure costs, political resistance, legal constraints, and the unique economics of an island system.

Josephine’s focus

  • Diagnosis of the existing system as a linear economy: extract, manufacture, discard, with heavy import dependence driving packaging volume and limited landfill capacity closing off the disposal path
  • The structural market conditions any solution has to work within: geographic isolation and high transport costs, limited competition and monopoly-like conditions, weak private incentives to invest in recycling, and the public-good and free-rider problems in environmental quality
  • Design and production of the team’s final presentation

Economic concepts applied

  • Market failure
  • Negative externalities
  • Public goods
  • Free-rider problem
  • Transaction costs
  • Regulatory failure
  • Circular economy

03ECON 317  ·  Fall 2025  ·  Collaborative research presentation  ·  Pecha Kucha format

Overfishing as a Common-Pool Resource Failure: Japan’s Fisheries

Common-pool resources  /  Market failure  /  Property rights  /  Policy sequencing

The question

Why does overfishing persist in Japan despite clear long-term costs, and in what order should the available remedies be applied?

The approach

Framed Japan’s fisheries decline, with production down to roughly a third of its 1984 peak, as a common-pool resource problem: rival in consumption, hard to exclude users from, and governed by weak long-term property rights. Evaluated four standard remedies against that diagnosis: total allowable catch quotas, individual transferable quotas, marine protected areas, and monitoring and enforcement.

Each layer is unenforceable without the one beneath it. Monitoring comes first; protected areas last.

Key finding

The four remedies are not substitutes competing on merit. They depend on one another, and the sequence decides whether any of them works at all. Quota systems require trustworthy catch data, so monitoring has to come first. Tradable rights require enforceable quotas. Protected areas are unenforceable without both.

Josephine’s focus

  • The comparative evaluation, and the argument that “which solution is best” is the wrong question
  • The implementation sequence: monitoring and enforcement, then expanded catch limits, then transferable quotas and property rights, then protected areas
  • The dependency case for each step, including why Japan’s existing but narrow quota system makes expansion the cheaper second move

Economic concepts applied

  • Common-pool resources
  • Tragedy of the commons
  • Property rights
  • Natural capital
  • Market failure
  • Quota systems
  • Tradable permits
  • Policy sequencing

04LLEA 342, German Studies  ·  Fall 2025  ·  Solo research presentation

Economic Policy as an Instrument of Control: The Nazi Economy, 1933–1945

Economic history  /  State control  /  Political economy  /  Labour markets

The question

How did the Nazi regime use economic policy as an instrument of ideology, social control and preparation for war rather than as an end in itself?

The approach

Traced economic policy from the 1933 crisis through the war economy, testing the regime’s public claims against what its policies actually did. Examined the public works programmes and the Autobahn, the Reich Labour Service, the abolition of independent unions, Strength Through Joy, the 1936 Four-Year Plan and the pursuit of autarky, the relationship between private industry and the state, forced labour, and the expropriation of Jewish-owned business and property.

The regime’s own framing set against what each programme did. Economic policy as an instrument, not an end.

Key finding

The recovery statistics the regime used to legitimize itself do not survive examination. Unemployment fell in part because women, Jews and political opponents were removed from the count and because labour conscription absorbed workers; the Autobahn was symbolically powerful but economically minor next to rearmament. Industry remained in private hands and profited from state contracts and suppressed labour, so the system was not an alternative to capitalism but a reshaping of it under authoritarian control. What resulted depended on debt, plunder and continued expansion, which left it structurally unable to survive peace.

  • Command economy
  • Autarky
  • Rearmament spending
  • Labour market intervention
  • State-business relations
  • Expropriation
  • Political economy

05LLEA 415, German Studies  ·  Spring 2026  ·  Solo research presentation

Planned and Market Economies: East and West Germany, 1949–1990

Comparative systems  /  Transition economics  /  Currency policy  /  Privatization

The question

What did four decades of divergent economic systems, and then a rapid reunification, do to the two Germanys?

The approach

Compared the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic on economic system, standard of living and trade, including the GDR’s substantial dependence on West German trade, credit and tariff-free access, which complicates any picture of two sealed economies. Then followed the mechanics of reunification: the 1990 currency union, the Treuhandanstalt privatization programme, and the fiscal burden carried by the West. Used Good Bye, Lenin! (2003) as a check on what aggregate figures leave out.

A conversion rate that was politically necessary and economically damaging, and what followed from it.

Key finding

The most consequential decision was the currency conversion. Converting Ostmark wages and savings to Deutsche Mark at 1:1 was politically necessary and economically damaging: it priced East German output out of its own market more or less overnight, and the Treuhandanstalt then transferred or closed more than 8,000 enterprises into that disadvantage. Political reunification took a day. Economic reunification is still unfinished, and the wage and wealth gap outlived the institutions that produced it.

Economic concepts applied

  • Planned versus market economies
  • Currency union
  • Exchange rate policy
  • Privatization
  • Transition economics
  • Regional convergence
  • Fiscal transfers
